## Executive assessment

The current upper-floor plan is a disciplined and largely coherent coordination model,
but it is stronger as a parametric programme-and-interface matrix than as fully resolved
domestic architecture. Its overall organization is viable; however, a substantial round
of spatial, sectional and technical development is required before the plan should be
frozen.

The design successfully preserves the front double-height hall, accommodates four
ensuite bedrooms, makes the primary suite dominant, supports two-phase delivery and
provides an acoustically enclosed private upper floor. Its main weaknesses are the
fragmented family centre, the route to Child Suite 1 through the mini deck, incomplete
demonstration of the wellness relaxation programme, unresolved life safety, and the
absence of a selected structural and services strategy.

## 2. Overall plan reading

P2 correctly occupies the rear **18.00 × 15.00 m = 270.00 m²** rectangle. It preserves
the front double-height volume and organizes:

- Phase 1: primary suite, Child Suite 1, family centre, mini deck and protected-stair
  study;
- Phase 2: Child Suite 2, guest suite and wellness/sauna;
- one isolatable construction boundary at `Y=11.00 m`;
- the complete P2-W04 acoustic enclosure toward the hall at `X=21.00 m`; and
- the 300 mm nominal P2-W05 residential envelope on the other three exterior edges.

The phasing strategy is legible and potentially economical. Future work is grouped in
one continuous band that can be isolated without fragmenting the entire floor.

## 3. Architectural strengths

### 3.1 Programme and hierarchy

The plan retains exactly four permanent suites, each with a private bathroom. The
primary suite is clearly dominant:

| Component | Gross area | Assessment |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Primary bedroom | 31.1 m² | Meets the intended dominant-bedroom range |
| Primary bathroom | 17.6 m² | Meets the programme minimum |
| Dressing/filter | 17.6 m² | Exceeds the original dressing target |

The corner bedroom, with glazing on two exterior edges, has the potential to become the
best private room in the house.

### 3.2 Measured child-bedroom equivalence

The current model reports:

- Child 1 bedroom: **22.07 m² net**;
- Child 2 bedroom: **21.65 m² net**;
- difference: **0.42 m²**; and
- proportions: **1.05:1** and **1.21:1**.

These figures satisfy D-042 and avoid residual or excessively elongated bedrooms.

### 3.3 Privacy from the hall and workshops

P2-W04 appropriately corrects the risk of treating P2 as an open gallery over the
workshops. The mini deck becomes the only scheduled visual connection. This is a strong
conceptual move: it preserves the great hall while protecting the privacy of the upper
floor.

### 3.4 Exterior-envelope intent

P2-W05 is consistent with the project hierarchy. Corrugated metal remains part of the
industrial exterior, while the private interior becomes smooth, quiet and domestic. The
double-frame principle can also accommodate insulation and services without exposing
them in bedrooms or circulation.

## 4. Material spatial issues

### 4.1 The family centre is fragmented

The centre of the plan is divided among:

- a 6.00 × 2.50 m family lounge/library;
- a 4.80 × 1.10 m family gallery/library;
- the private hall;
- the protected arrival; and
- the mini deck.

The combined allowance is generous, but usable space is dispersed among several small
rectangles. A 2.50 m-deep room is shallow for a genuine family lounge containing seating,
circulation, books and simultaneous activities. The 1.10 m gallery is effectively a
corridor rather than an occupiable library.

The centre therefore reads more as a sequence of circulation zones than as the
recognizable family heart of P2.

**Recommendation:** redesign these components as one coherent shared room, with
circulation absorbed along its edges. A fitted library wall could replace the dedicated
1.10 m strip.

### 4.2 Child Suite 1 is reached through the mini deck

The only entrance to Child Bedroom 1 connects it to the mini deck. This makes a
contemplative room overlooking the hall into the compulsory anteroom of a bedroom.

Consequences include:

- reduced privacy for the child;
- circulation through the principal viewing space;
- competition between everyday access and quiet use of the deck; and
- greater sensitivity to noise at the hall-facing acoustic edge.

**Recommendation:** give Child Suite 1 access from the family centre or a dedicated
filter. The mini deck should be a destination, not a corridor.

### 4.3 Child-bedroom equality is numeric, but not yet experiential

Areas and proportions comply, but the two rooms have different relationships:

- Child 1 is connected to the family centre and mini deck;
- Child 2 opens directly from the long Phase 2 lobby;
- their window lengths differ; and
- they occupy opposite sides of the roof and have different acoustic adjacencies.

This is not necessarily unacceptable, but equivalence in daylight, view, storage,
privacy, perceived height and acoustic exposure has not yet been demonstrated.

### 4.4 Primary-suite circulation requires development

The suite is entered through the dressing/filter. This provides privacy, but every daily
entry and exit passes through the dressing area.

The bathroom is an L-shaped room formed by a 4.80 × 2.40 m wet salon and a
1.90 × 3.20 m service band connected through a 1.00 m opening. The concept is plausible,
but remains diagrammatic:

- privacy among the WC, shower and basins is not demonstrated;
- the 1.90 m gross band may become congested after finishes and fittings;
- storage, drying, ventilation and simultaneous use are not tested; and
- the large bedroom has not been validated with a complete furniture and activity plan.

A 1:25 layout is required before treating the suite as resolved.

### 4.5 The guest suite is below the nominal gross target

The current guest suite is approximately **29.4 m² gross**, compared with the earlier
control target of approximately **33 m²**. Its bedroom is approximately **14.3 m² net**,
although the gross room rectangle remains within the current coordinated band.

The suite is habitable but less generous than the original brief. Its entry filter uses
the declared minimum **1.20 m clear** width, and the wardrobe is reached only through the
bedroom.

The owner should explicitly confirm whether this reduction in spatial quality is
acceptable before design freeze.

### 4.6 The wellness experience is incomplete

The 2.90 × 5.55 m reserve accommodates a sauna and shower, but the plan does not
demonstrate the relaxation area required by hard rule 21. The room is linear and opens
directly from the Phase 2 lobby.

The developed layout should provide or identify:

- a dry cooling or reclining area;
- changing space;
- towel and equipment storage;
- separation of wet and dry circulation;
- privacy from the lobby; and
- ventilation and humidity control.

The family lounge should not be assumed to perform this role without an explicit design
decision and a credible route between the spaces.

## 5. Circulation and egress

The parametric access graph demonstrates that every enclosed room connects to the stair.
This is useful evidence, but it is not a life-safety approval.

The principal stair is currently the only recognizable route. The rear retractable stair
is only a geometric reserve and introduces unresolved risks:

- mechanical failure or poor maintenance;
- manual operation under smoke, darkness or power loss;
- undefined slope, landings, guards, handrails and headroom;
- unverified exterior discharge and fire exposure; and
- unverified separation from the primary exit.

The label “protected stair” also does not establish a tested fire-resisting enclosure,
door set, structural system or junction strategy.

**Critical priority:** complete the fire and life-safety concept for residential
accommodation above the technical hall before freezing the plan. The alternatives should
include a simple, secure fixed second stair for comparison with the retractable system.

## 6. Daylight, views and privacy

Every bedroom has an exterior window, which is positive. The current glazing strategy is
nevertheless aggressive:

- bedroom windows are shown up to 2.70 m high;
- the study sill is only 0.10 m;
- the primary bedroom has approximately 8 m of glazing across two façades; and
- the actual site orientation is not known.

This may create thermal loss, condensation, glare, privacy problems and unnecessary
cost. Low sills also require coordinated fall protection and opening restrictions.

The protected arrival, family lounge and Phase 2 lobby are comparatively internal and
depend on borrowed light from the mini deck or open doors. They may feel dark and deep
when the suites are closed.

## 7. Acoustic assessment

The twin-frame wall concept is credible, but its actual performance depends on
unresolved details:

- acoustic doors and threshold seals;
- roof, floor and return junctions;
- MEP penetrations;
- structural and floor-borne transmission;
- vibration from the workshops; and
- the combined P2-W04, exposed-truss and GLZ-DECK interface.

Child Suite 1 is particularly sensitive because of its adjacency to the deck and
hall-facing edge. The complete floor build-up requires acoustic and vibration design;
vertical partitions alone cannot control workshop transmission.

## 8. Structure and constructability

Structural resolution remains one of the highest project risks:

- the large truss at `X=21.00 m` is not designed;
- the truss must coexist with P2-W04 without puncturing it or creating acoustic bridges;
- the rear structural strategy still includes a 4.50 m overhang hypothesis rather than
  a selected system;
- the four stair-corner columns are geometric reservations only;
- existing structural allowances have not been reconciled with the measured masses and
  wind reactions of P2-W01, P2-W04 and P2-W05; and
- metal deck, vibration, diaphragm action, joints, fire protection and foundations
  remain unresolved.

Beam depth, supports, bracing and service zones may materially change the architectural
geometry.

## 9. Services and wet-area coordination

The relocation of the laundry to PB creates a potentially efficient vertical
relationship with the primary wet band. Nevertheless, the plan does not yet show:

- sanitary and ventilation shafts;
- drain routes and falls;
- maintenance access;
- extract systems for four bathrooms and the sauna;
- hot-water generation and circulation;
- wellness dehumidification; or
- interfaces between waterproofing and the structure.

The wet rooms remain relatively dispersed. A coordinated MEP core and riser plan should
precede consolidation of the partitions.

## 10. Cost and design efficiency

P2 concentrates several potentially expensive components:

- four private bathrooms;
- sauna and additional shower;
- 250 mm twin-frame acoustic partitions;
- a 300 mm double-frame exterior envelope;
- large windows;
- specialist acoustic glazing;
- one singular exposed truss; and
- a potentially bespoke retractable stair.

Essential performance should not be reduced. Savings should instead be pursued through:

- repeated bathroom modules and details;
- aligned shafts and fixtures;
- standardized window sizes;
- simplification of the family centre;
- elimination of residual circulation space;
- comparison of the retractable stair with a simple fixed solution; and
- design of the truss and acoustic enclosure as one coordinated interface.

These recommendations identify value-engineering studies only. They do not change the
active cost baseline.

## Conclusion

The proposal has a strong conceptual basis. It preserves the great hall, retains four
ensuite bedrooms, gives the primary suite appropriate hierarchy, supports phased
construction and protects the private upper floor acoustically.

Its principal weakness is that geometric optimization has progressed further than the
domestic experience. The family centre remains fragmented, Child Suite 1 has a
questionable access route, the wellness zone does not yet demonstrate relaxation space,
and egress remains unresolved.

R12 should remain the active coordination hypothesis, but it should not be frozen. The
next revision should concentrate on the family centre, Child Suite 1 access, life safety
and the structural/acoustic section. No additional programme is recommended until those
fundamental issues are resolved.
